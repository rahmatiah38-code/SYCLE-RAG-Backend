The effect of air pollution exposure on menstrual cycle health using self-reported data from a mobile health app: a prospective, observational study

Summary

Background

Toxicological evidence suggests that ambient air pollution has endocrine-disrupting properties that can affect menstrual cycle functioning, which represents an important marker of women's reproductive health. We aimed to estimate the effect of short-term and long-term PM2·5 exposure on menstrual cycle outcomes across the USA, Brazil, and Mexico using self-reported data from a mobile health app.

Methods

For this prospective observational study, we collected de-identified self-reported data from the Clue mobile health app, in which users self-tracked menstruation cycles. For the current study, eligible participants were aged 18–44 years, were not using hormonal birth control, and lived in one of 230 cities in the USA, Mexico, or Brazil. The primary outcome of interest at the city level was the proportion of menstrual cycles with abnormally short length (<24 days) and long length (>38 days) of all cycles recorded. The primary outcome at the cycle level was a binary indicator: abnormal cycle length (<24 days or >38 days) or not (normal cycle length). We used regression analyses to evaluate associations between long-term PM2·5 concentrations (mean concentration between 2016 and 2020) and the city-level outcomes after controlling for potential confounders. Conditional logistic regression models were used to evaluate associations between cycle-specific PM2·5 and if a cycle was of abnormal length within an individual in the dataset, after controlling for time-varying factors.

Findings

Between Jan 1, 2016 and Dec 31, 2020, 92 550 app users residing in 230 cities across the USA, Brazil, and Mexico provided data corresponding to 2 220 281 menstrual cycles, and were included in our main cohort. A significant association was observed between long-term PM2·5 exposure and the proportion of menstrual cycles of abnormally long or short duration (odds ratio [OR] 1·023 [95% CI 1·013–1·033]) and the proportion of cycles that were specifically abnormally long (OR 1·036 [1·023–1·049]) for every 10 μg/m3 increase in PM2·5. No associations were identified between short-term PM2·5 concentrations and abnormal cycle length.

Interpretation

These findings suggest that PM2·5 exposure affects menstrual cycle outcomes. More research is needed to better elucidate the biological mechanisms through which PM2·5 affects the menstrual cycle.

Introduction

Air pollution is one of the largest environmental health risks globally.1 Epidemiological evidence has linked PM2·5 with premature mortality and a multitude of morbidities.2, 3 Increasing evidence indicates associations between air pollution and reproductive health outcomes such as preterm birth,4 low birthweight,5 shortened umbilical cord telomere length,6 and neonatal and infant mortality.7 To date, few studies have investigated the effect of air pollution on reproductive health, separate to pregnancy and fertility.8, 9, 10 Only a small number of studies have investigated the effects of pollution on the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis, which controls the menstrual cycle.11, 12, 13
Regular menstrual cycles reflect normal functioning of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis, a vital indicator of women's health.14, 15 Research has shown that highly variable menstrual cycles can be associated with an increased risk of premature mortality,16 cardiovascular disease,17 type 2 diabetes,18 cancer,19 and infertility.20 Toxicological evidence suggests that menstrual cycle regularity is affected by exposure to pollution.21, 22 However, to our knowledge, only four epidemiological studies have evaluated this effect empirically.23, 24, 25, 26 None of these studies have considered the impact of PM2·5 exposure on menstrual cycle outcomes, despite a substantial amount of evidence suggesting that PM2·5 exposure has a more severe impact on health than any other air pollutant.1
The collection of data through mobile health apps has provided an opportunity27 to better understand a diverse range of individuals across geographies. The rise in popularity of menstrual trackers, which are the second most popular app for adolescent girls and the fourth most popular for adult women,28 is starting to enable access to large-scale, longitudinal cycle data for the study of factors influencing features of the menstrual cycle. To our knowledge, no studies have yet used this type of mobile health data to investigate the effect of air pollution on the menstrual cycle. In this study, we aimed to fill these evidence gaps by assessing the effect of both short-term and long-term PM2·5 exposure on menstrual cycle outcomes using data from a mobile health tracking app. To our knowledge, this represents the largest study of air pollution and menstrual cycle outcomes to date in middle-income and high-income countries, and across high and low PM2·5 environments.
Research in context
Evidence before this study
Regular menstrual cycles are a vital indicator of women's health. However, the effect of air pollution on menstrual cycle health has been understudied. We searched PubMed and Google Scholar for epidemiological studies published in English between Jan 1, 1970 and Jan 1, 2024 using the key words: “menstrual”, “pollution”, “air quality”, and “environmental”. We supplemented our findings with those from systematic reviews, and identified only four previous epidemiological studies that have evaluated associations between long-term exposure to air pollutants and menstrual cycle outcomes. Findings from these studies suggest that acute exposure to air pollution can affect menstrual cycle length by disrupting follicular recruitment in the follicular phase or follicular conversion to the corpus luteum in the luteal phase.
Added value of this study
No previous studies have considered associations between menstrual cycle outcomes and PM2·5 specifically. Four previous studies that assessed associations between other pollutants and menstrual cycle outcomes were done in a single city or country and relied on small samples. Our study is the first to examine the association between short-term and long-term exposure to PM2·5 and menstrual cycle health outcomes. Using data from a mobile health tracking app, our study is also the first to investigate the associations between pollution and menstrual health. The data, which were self-reported in the Clue app, include information about millions of menstrual cycles, recorded across three countries. We developed a novel approach to process and analyse these data, providing a framework for future environmental health studies using data obtained from menstrual tracking apps.
Implications of all the available evidence
We identified associations between long-term exposure to PM2·5 and menstrual cycle outcomes. Our results are consistent with previous epidemiological and toxicological evidence suggesting that air pollution might affect menstrual health. Considering the limitations of self-tracked data, more studies are needed to replicate this finding. More research is also needed to understand the mechanisms by which PM2·5 affects menstrual cycle health.

Methods

Study design and participants

For this prospective observational study, we collected de-identified self-reported data from the Clue app, developed by BioWink (Berlin, Germany), comprising a total of 4 599 339 menstrual cycles tracked between Jan 1, 2016 and Dec 31, 2020, from 190 254 users. For the current study, eligible participants were aged 18–44 years, were not using hormonal birth control, and lived in one of 230 cities across continental USA, Mexico, or Brazil. The dataset included user age bracketed into 5-year intervals, birth control method used, and BMI where available. Data on race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status were not collected in the app.
Users of the Clue app can self-track menstrual cycle experiences over time. Here, we focused on reported menstruation data (ie, users’ self-reports of the days they experience menses). We defined menses as sequential days of tracked bleeding (greater than spotting) unbroken by no more than 1 day in which only spotting or no bleeding occurred. We defined menstrual cycle length as the number of days from the first day of menstrual bleeding through to and including the day before the first day of the next menstrual flow. Clue restricts the minimum cycle duration to 10 days. To define our main cohort, we built on previous studies that have used Clue data29 and filtered the data (figure 1). A detailed description of this process is described in the appendix (p 3).
Clue users are not required to specify gender. In this study, we refer to Clue users or menstruators as women, but we acknowledge that not all menstruators identify as women and vice versa. The Clue app data were not collected specifically for this study and none of the study team had access to identifiers linked to the data. These activities do not meet the regulatory definition of human subjects research and thus the need for ethical approval was waived by the institutional review board of the University of Colorado Denver (Denver, CO, USA). All data used were fully de-identified. All users in the dataset consented to the use of their data for scientific research purposes.30 Our study adhered to STROBE reporting guidelines.

Procedures

We also considered two supplementary cohorts, defined by: (1) restricting the dataset to periods where individuals had sustained engagement with the app and no menstrual cycles longer than 90 days. These parameters were chosen based on previous research using the Clue dataset.29 Although this duration might not strictly define amenorrhea according to current International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) guidelines,31 as per the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and previous FIGO guidelines, a gap of three months (90 days) without a period might warrant further investigation to rule out underlying causes. We acknowledge that app users might experience unusually long cycles or amenorrhea. However, app usage data suggest that such long cycles are more likely to be due to inconsistent app use or discontinuation of app tracking, rather than true physiological absence of menstruation. Therefore, we applied a 90-day cutoff, in accordance with the definition of amenorrhea of the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists and previous research,32 to minimise the risk of including potentially erroneous datapoints due to lapses in tracking; (2) restricting the cohort to users for whom at least 1 year of data were available and restricting the number of cycles considered for each user to the cycles recorded as having started in the first year of using the app to standardise the time considered across all users.
Full details on supplementary cohorts definitions are in the appendix (p 2). We present the results for our main cohort in the main text and results for the supplementary cohorts are included in the appendix (pp 12–16).

We assessed long-term menstrual cycle outcomes (city level) and short-term menstrual cycle outcomes (cycle level; table 1).

The primary outcome of interest at the city level was the proportion of cycles with abnormally short length (<24 days) and long length (>38 days)17, 18 of all cycles recorded, as separate outcomes for each of the 230 cities in the dataset. The secondary outcomes at the city level were the proportion of cycles in each city that were short (< 24 days) and long (> 38 days).
The primary outcome at the cycle level was a binary indicator: abnormal cycle length (<24 days or >38 days) or not (normal cycle length), with frequent (short; <24 days) or prolonged (long; >38 days) menstrual cycles defined as 1,17, 18 and menstrual cycles of any other length defined as 0. No well-established methods are available to define clinically relevant menstrual cycle outcomes from app data. Therefore, we used normal menstrual cycle length definitions as per the FIGO guidelines.31
Clue assigned app users to the city in which they had opened the app at least 70% of the time, based on Internet Protocol address. To enhance data security, only users in cities with at least 100 users, who also had similar baseline characteristics (including age range, BMI range, and last contraception method used), were included in the Clue dataset. To link PM2·5 concentrations experienced by users with menstrual cycle outcomes, we used publicly accessible shapefiles of city, town, and municipality boundaries provided by the US Census Bureau, the Mexican National Institute of Statistics and Geography, and the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (appendix p 5).
We joined each city with mean monthly temperature from ERA-5 Land monthly mean ECMF Climate Reanalysis data and annual normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) from the Landsat 8 Collection 1 Tier 1, composited using Google Earth Engine. We also joined each city with widely used monthly mean PM2·5 concentrations (μg/m3), generated by researchers at Washington University (St Louis, MO, USA), derived from combining data from satellite instruments, chemical transport models, and ground-based monitors using geophysical relationships between surface PM2.5 and satellite-derived aerosol optical depth.33 Previous global evaluations showed good agreement between the satellite-derived estimates and ground-based monitoring data (R2 0·91).33 We chose NDVI and temperature because they have been shown to be associated with reproductive health.34, 35
Long-term exposures in each city were calculated as averages of monthly temperature and PM2·5 concentrations between 2016 and 2020. The mean annual NDVI values between 2016 and 2020 for each city were used to represent long-term city-level NDVI. The long-term exposures were thus the same for all individuals in each city in this analysis. Secondary engagement-weighted long-term environmental exposures used in the supplementary analyses are in the appendix (p 6).
No previous data exist regarding the specific window of susceptibility of the menstrual cycle functioning to air pollution; therefore, we defined short-term exposures as those experienced over a cycle length as has been done previously.24 We assigned short-term exposures within each cycle in our dataset a weighted average of exposures of monthly mean temperature, NDVI, and PM2·5, using the number of days in each month that the cycle extended over as weights. For example, if a cycle spanned multiple months (eg, between March 16 and April 17, 2016), we assigned exposure to temperature and PM2·5 during the cycle as follows: monthly mean temperature and PM2·5 in March 2016 × 16 + temperature (PM2·5) in April 2016 × 17/(16 + 17), where 16 and 17 correspond to the number of days in March and April that the cycle included. For NDVI, if a cycle spanned multiple years, for example between Dec 25, 2017, and Jan 27, 2018, we assigned exposure to NDVI as NDVI in 2017 × 7 + NDVI in 2018 × 27 / (7 +  27), where 7 and 27 corresponded to the number of days in 2017 and 2018 that the cycle included. We defined a secondary short-term exposure to address the challenge of our main exposure being dependent on cycle length (appendix p 6).
Socioeconomic data were not available for users of Clue, although evidence suggests that users of health apps are younger, with a higher income, and more educated than the general population.36, 37 We used city-level socioeconomic status data from government-administered surveys for each of the 230 cities in the dataset. Specifically, we considered: (1) the proportion of residents living in poverty and (2) the proportion of residents who smoke. These factors were selected because they have been shown to be associated with menstrual cycle outcomes.11, 38 The definition and sources of these socioeconomic status data, and how they were harmonised across countries are described in the appendix (p 7). Other potential confounders not considered in this study due to the paucity of data were diet, exercise, shift work, alcohol consumption, marijuana consumption, race and ethnicity, stress, endocrine-disrupting chemicals, such as perfluoroalkyl substances, breastfeeding, and age at menarche.11

Statistical analysis

We estimated associations between city-level menstrual health outcomes (proportions) and long-term PM2·5, after controlling for long-term exposures to temperature and NDVI, and socioeconomic status variables: proportion of smokers, proportion of residents living in poverty, and fixed effects at the country level using a logistic regression:

where πij = (PYij = 1), where PY is the probability of Yij being either 0 or 1, which was a binary indicator of an abnormal menstrual cycle length for the jth individual in the ith city. Because all covariates were measured at the city level, the πij was the same for all individuals within a city. Then, assuming that individuals within cities are independent, this model can be equivalently represented as a collapsed city-level model of binomial proportions. Thus, we implemented all analyses and conducted inference at the city level. We included temperature as a natural cubic spline, where we allowed the degree of freedom of the splines to vary from 1 to 5 and chose the degree of freedom that resulted in the best model fit using the Akaike Information Criterion. We found that 1 degree of freedom yielded the lowest Akaike Information Criterion.
We estimated the short-term association between cycle-specific PM2·5 and the cycle-level binary outcome: abnormal cycle length or not, within an individual in the dataset after controlling for time-varying temperature and NDVI:

where Yji is the outcome: abnormal cycle length or not, for the ith cycle in the jth individual. We used a conditional logistic model to evaluate the association. A conditional logistic model is an extension of logistic regression that produces adjusted odds ratios (ORs) that are conditional on the group to which an observation belongs (ie, the app user). By conditioning on app users, the model eliminates across-user confounding due to time-invariant unobserved variables such as health status, smoking status, and sleep patterns, that could also affect menstrual health. Not all users experienced an abnormal cycle. For a user's data to be included in the conditional logistic regression, the user must have had at least one normal and one abnormal cycle. Therefore, the number of users considered in this analysis were further limited to 77 073 users from our main cohort (83·2%) who had at least one normal cycle and one abnormal cycle. Data for 1 964 490 menstrual cycles (88·5%) were ultimately retained for this analysis.
In previous studies, temperature has been shown to have a non-linear effect on health; therefore, we included this variable as natural cubic splines. We found that 5 degrees of freedom resulted in the lowest Akaike Information Criterion. We present the associations evaluated with robust SEs clustered at the user level to account for correlations between outcomes within each user. Secondary analyses, including stepwise addition of covariates, evaluating the lagged impact of short-term PM2·5 exposure on menstrual cycle outcomes, and subgroup-specific analyses are described in the appendix (p 9).
All analyses were done using R (version 4.2.1). We report all results as the change in menstrual health outcome (95% CI) per 10 μg/m3 increase in cycle-specific PM2·5. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and p values of less than 0·05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Between Jan 1, 2016 and Dec 31, 2020, 92 550 app users residing in 230 cities in three countries provided data corresponding to 2 220 281 menstrual cycles, and were included in our main cohort. Initial descriptive analyses suggested that 388 789 (17·5%) of 2 220 281 recorded cycles were abnormal in length, varying between 16·6% and 18·3% for the individual countries (table 2). Most abnormal cycles were long (around 10% of all cycles). At the city level, the mean proportion of abnormal cycles ranged between 16·9% and 19·7% (table 3).

Substantial differences in the mean city-level PM2·5 concentrations were observed between the USA (mean 8·0 μg/m3 [1·6]), Brazil (13·7 μg/m3 [4·7]), and Mexico (16·4 μg/m3 [6·0]; table 3). Moderate correlations were identified between PM2·5 and the proportion of smokers and proportion of users living in poverty at the city level overall (–0·18 and 0·16, respectively), and in the USA (–0·17 and 0·08), Brazil (0·13 and –0·06), and in Mexico (–0·11 and 0·19; appendix p 8).
In logistic regression models controlling for several socioeconomic status parameters, significant associations were identified between long-term PM2·5 concentrations (averaged between 2016 and 2020) at the city level and the proportion of abnormal cycles (OR 1·023 [95% CI 1·013–1·033]) and proportion of long cycles (OR 1·036 [1·023–1·049]; figure 3A). These results were robust when assessing secondary outcomes, when using an engagement-weighted exposure, and when considering supplementary cohorts (appendix p 12).
No associations were identified between short-term PM2·5 concentrations and abnormal cycle lengths (OR 0·999 [95% CI 0·988–1·011]), short cycle lengths (1·002 [0·986–1·020]), or long cycle lengths (0·998 [0·983–1·012]). Similar results were observed in supplementary analyses (appendix p 13). PM2·5 concentrations experienced by users in their three previous menstrual cycles had a significant effect on the presence of abnormal cycle lengths and abnormally long cycle lengths (appendix p 16). Although the 95% CIs of the overall effect of PM2·5 concentrations during the current cycle and the three previous cycles crossed 1 (appendix p 13), our result suggests that more research is needed to evaluate the crucial window of exposure to short-term PM2·5 concentrations on menstrual cycle outcomes. No associations were identified between short-term PM2·5 and abnormal cycle length in any subgroup (defined by birth year range, BMI, and country; appendix p 13).

Discussion

Our study is the first to examine the association between short-term and long-term exposures to PM2.5 and various menstrual cycle health outcomes. We observed a significant association between long-term PM2·5 levels and the proportion of abnormal cycles experienced by app users. Although the effect size observed in this study is small and strictly suggestive, it is consistent with the wider literature that indicates that long-term exposure to pollution can affect menstrual health. Specifically, other studies have also found that long-term exposure to pollution affects menstrual disorders, such as menstrual irregularity.26, 39
A review of the existing evidence suggests that acute exposure to air pollution can affect menstrual cycle length by disrupting follicular recruitment in the follicular phase or the transition from follicle to corpus luteum in the luteal phase.12 A major contributor to variability in menstrual cycle length is the time required to recruit a dominant follicle for ovulation, which typically takes around 14 days. However, air pollution can affect this time period in two ways: (1) air pollution might alter oestradiol production by disrupting cholesterol uptake into the theca cells of the follicle, or by impairing aromatase activity or granulosa cell function, which are essential for oestradiol synthesis. This, in turn, affects the high oestradiol microenvironment required for the development of the dominant follicle and ultimately ovulation; (2) air pollution might also interfere with receptor-mediated oestrogen signalling at any level of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis, thereby disrupting follicle development. Air pollution can affect the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, which also typically lasts around 14 days, by increasing the rate of corpus luteum degradation. The corpus luteum is crucial for progesterone production and support of the endometrial lining, and its premature degradation can lead to a shortened luteal phase.12
In this study, long-term PM2·5 concentrations were primarily associated with the proportion of abnormally long cycles. We present a hypothesis for why this might be the case, although more work is needed to understand the systemic and cellular pathways by which air pollution impacts menstrual cycle health. Folliculogenesis is a continuous process, meaning that at any time the ovary contains follicles in many stages of development. Most follicles die and never complete development. A small number of follicles (over a lifetime) develop fully to produce a secondary oocyte (egg), which is released when the follicle ruptures, in a process called ovulation. Typically, only one oocyte matures and is released during a single menstrual cycle: this maturation and release prompts other developing follicles to die, but multiple sets of cohorts of developing follicles (at different stages) will still be developing if the single lucky oocyte does not become fertilised. A short-term exposure to elevated levels of pollution might be less impactful to the follicular phase since there are more follicles present to continue. Long-term exposure to elevated levels of pollution might prevent multiple cohorts of follicles from proceeding.
No associations were identified between cycle-specific PM2·5 concentrations and abnormal cycle length. It is possible that exposure misclassification (due to coarse spatial and temporal exposure aggregations) resulted in attenuated associations in the cycle-specific analyses. However, PM2·5 is relatively spatially and temporally homogeneous compared with other air pollutants; therefore, we hypothesise that the influence of these factors should be modest. Moreover, this approach to exposure classification is common in air pollution epidemiology and has permitted identification of associations between air pollution and many other health outcomes. Previous research has also found stronger effects of long-term PM2·5 exposure on most health outcomes than short-term exposure.40, 41, 42
The effect of PM2.5 on menstrual cycle outcomes has not been empirically evaluated in humans. Only four studies (appendix p 18) have investigated the effects of pollution on menstrual cycle outcomes; however, none of these studies specifically examined PM2·5, and all were based on relatively small sample sizes. We did not consider the pollutants used in these studies (PM10, total suspended particles, SO2, NO2) due to the scarcity of widespread satellite-derived estimates of these exposures. Moreover, widespread epidemiological evidence suggests that PM2.5 has a stronger impact on human health than any other pollutant.
This study provides a framework for future research using menstrual tracker data to investigate factors associated with abnormal menstrual cycle outcomes. In particular, we demonstrated how to establish a cohort using menstrual cycle data and how to construct so-called sensitivity cohorts to assess possible sources of bias due to disengagement and other complex challenges associated with mobile health apps. We carefully defined menstrual outcomes consistent with FIGO guidelines both at the cycle level and aggregated across a city, which can be applied more broadly.
This study must be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, self-reported data might be unreliable for several reasons, such as inconsistent user engagement and users forgetting to track their menses. We attempted to account for this by filtering the data to define our main cohort using the flowchart. Additionally, some users might not have tracked the use of hormonal birth control during the period of this study, potentially resulting in some cycles being inaccurately classified as natural and thereby included in this analysis. Finally, we did not have information on whether the users in our sample were lactating or using other types of hormones or medications that could impact their menstrual cycles.
Second, the monthly PM2·5 concentrations considered in this study are derived from satellite data and thus have several uncertainties. However, the modelled estimates perform well, with a cross-validated R2 of 0·76,33 and are widely used in global epidemiological research.7, 43 Furthermore, due to privacy reasons, the spatial resolution in this study was coarse, being at the city level. Considering the spatial resolution of our dataset, the long-term, city-level exposures are more reliable than short-term, individual-level exposures, which makes our identification of significant associations between long-term PM2·5 and city-level outcomes particularly important and might explain why no significant associations were identified in the short term. Furthermore, using city-level PM2·5 as a proxy for personal exposure to PM2.5 from outdoor sources might have measurement error. Such measurement error could have unpredictable effects on our estimates of the effect of air pollution on the menstrual cycle outcomes considered. Without access to individual-level mobility data, it is impossible to estimate personal exposure levels. We note that we assigned users a city of residence based on where they logged into the app more than 70% of the time. Users could potentially have logged into the app when at work in a different city and thus this could contribute to further exposure misclassification. We cannot disentangle the effects of different particulate matter chemical composition and sources in our study, which are likely to vary across the study area due to different sources and differences in meteorology reports. Nevertheless, our exposure assignment strategy has been widely used in the field of air pollution epidemiology.
Third, when conducting city-specific analyses, we adjusted for socioeconomic status variables for the overall population of each city (proportion of smokers and living in poverty). Because individual-level socioeconomic status and behavioural risk-factor data were not collected by Clue, we were unable to control for measures of these potential confounders among Clue users specifically. Place-based factors can affect health and potentially confound associations between PM2·5 and menstrual health. Previous research has suggested that neighbourhood-level socioeconomic status is a more important confounder of PM2·5 health effects than individual socioeconomic status.44 Moderate correlations were identified between PM2·5 and the proportion of smokers and proportion of users living in poverty at the city level, suggesting that controlling for the proportion of city residents who were smokers and living in poverty, to some extent, accounts for some confounding by place-based factors. Since our study is based in three different countries, we opted for these covariates because they have previously been shown to affect health,45, 46 and were measured in a comparable manner (appendix p 7). Despite controlling for these city-level potential confounders, we acknowledge that the results of our analysis could be subject to ecological bias and should be interpreted with awareness of the inherent limitations of ecological studies. However, considering the scarcity of previous research and the limited data to study this topic, the results of this analysis provide an important indicator that further research in this area is warranted. We acknowledge that the Clue app cohort is different from that of the general city population, in that, first, it comprises only women; second, it is likely younger and of higher socioeconomic status; and third, members likely live in specific locations within each city for which city-level data might not be representative. This could contribute to exposure or confounder misclassification in the city-level analyses, which could bias associations in either direction. However, studying a more homogeneous population (primarily younger with higher socioeconomic status) also reduces the potential for confounding by these factors. Considering our unique sample, it is possible that our results might not be generalisable to the broader populations of these three countries. Nevertheless, our analyses represent the most comprehensive study to date to investigate the effect of pollution on menstrual cycle health on a multinational scale.
Fourth, in our cycle-specific analyses, a key assumption was that after including individual-specific fixed effects, there were no temporal changes in the individual's behaviour that would likely impact the menstrual cycle outcomes considered in this study, such as sleep or BMI.
Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates the potential of mobile health data in advancing our understanding of the effect of air pollution and other environmental factors on the menstrual cycle. The data from the Clue app enabled the evaluation of the impact of PM2.5 on menstrual cycle outcomes across a wide range of exposure levels. Considering the scarcity of data regarding the role of environmental contributors to menstrual cycle outcomes, such databases offer a valuable opportunity to address these knowledge gaps. Our findings further substantiate the evidence that PM2.5 contributes to human health disparities, including effects on menstrual cycle health.
